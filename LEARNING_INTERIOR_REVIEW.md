# REI: research-led refinement of the existing interiors

18 October 2026. Scope: improve the existing Learn, lesson, Create and tool pages. No destinations, data stores or paid services added. Original optical homepage graphics remain.

## What the audit found

The first Learn/Create version delivered real functions, but the interiors mostly presented paragraphs in a single column. Students had to infer what each lesson would help them do; examples had limited visual structure; the original six answer keys all occupied the second position; a single familiar question could be answered by recognition without applying the idea elsewhere. Tool results appeared beneath the form, which forced scrolling between inputs and explanations. A long paper-result list separated source selection from the download control.

These are implementation observations, not findings from a student study. Research guides the changes below; it cannot tell us the best arrangement for REI without testing the actual students and tasks.

## Where information now belongs

| Existing surface | Placement and purpose | Why this location |
|---|---|---|
| Learning library | Lesson titles paired with an observable outcome; topic filters remain above the list | Helps students select by the decision they need to make rather than a generic lesson label |
| Lesson introduction | Brief problem and a single decision rule; goal in the adjacent desktop rail | Establishes relevance before asking for a response |
| Example | Explanation steps, a “what to notice” cue and a labeled organizer on one light reading surface | Keeps the relevant explanation together with the material being compared |
| Lesson orientation | Three section controls: example, decision, new situation; compact normal-flow layout on phones | Gives students a visible route without forcing page changes or locking later content |
| First challenge | Answer-specific feedback directly beneath its choices | Reduces the distance between the decision and its explanation |
| Second challenge | A new context on the same lesson page, followed by the same decision rule | Offers an additional application opportunity rather than another copy of the original question |
| Lesson end | Matching practical tool, optional sources, then next lesson | Places action after the student has seen what the idea is for |
| Create index | Each existing task states what students bring and what it produces | Makes choosing a tool depend on a recognizable input/output relationship |
| Tool desktop layout | Input on the left; output/preview alongside it | Keeps input decisions and their consequences close enough to compare |
| Tool phone layout | One column, inputs first, output next; generation moves focus to the result | Preserves reading order and makes generated content findable |
| Paper results | Selection count and export control stay alongside the result list | Makes selection/export status visible while reviewing records |
| Further detail | Optional expanded principle and source links | Keeps the core task visible while retaining depth |

## Evidence reviewed and the limits of applying it

1. **IES / What Works Clearinghouse, Organizing Instruction and Study to Improve Student Learning.** The guide recommends combining explanatory graphics and words, connecting concrete and abstract representations, alternating worked solutions and problems, and using explanatory questioning. REI uses a concrete example, a labeled conceptual organizer and subsequent decisions. This is an adaptation to student research education, not evidence that these REI lessons improve research skill. [Practice guide](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/1).

2. **Tamara van Gog, Signaling/Cueing Principle, Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning (2021).** The public chapter summary describes cues that direct attention to relevant information and organization, with boundary conditions. REI adds “what to notice,” concise outcome statements and labeled example parts. The particular visual arrangement has not been experimentally tested. [Chapter and DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108894333.022).

3. **Richard Mayer, Segmenting Principle, Multimedia Learning (2009).** The public chapter summary concerns user-paced segments, especially in complex, fast-paced multimedia for inexperienced learners. REI applies the related design idea of a visible, self-paced three-part structure; this is not a claim that section-jump controls reproduce the effects reported for narrated animations. [Chapter](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511811678.013).

4. **Pan & Rickard (2018), Transfer of test-enhanced learning.** The abstract describes a meta-analysis of retrieval-practice transfer and shows that transfer depends on task conditions. REI adds one different-context question per lesson to create another application opportunity. Two short choice questions do not measure independent research competence, and a successful answer does not prove broad transfer. [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29733621/).

5. **Education Endowment Foundation, Feedback toolkit.** The synthesis emphasizes feedback directed at the task and how to improve. REI retains answer-specific reasons and adds a reusable decision rule beside them, for both better and weaker choices. Much of EEF’s evidence concerns school settings; this is an adaptation, not an estimate of gains for university research learners. Do not apply its headline effect estimate to REI. [Feedback guidance](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/feedback).

6. **Nielsen Norman Group, Recognition and Recall in User Interfaces.** This practitioner guidance supports showing relevant choices and context rather than making users remember how to operate the interface. REI makes tool inputs/outputs and the lesson route visible. Ease of interface recognition is distinct from retrieval practice used to learn subject matter. [Article](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/recognition-and-recall/).

7. **Nielsen Norman Group, Progressive Disclosure.** This practitioner guidance recommends prioritizing primary information and revealing secondary detail when needed. REI keeps the example, rule and challenges visible, while longer explanation and sources remain expandable. Essential instructions must not be hidden in disclosure. This is a usability rationale, not a controlled educational-effect study. [Article](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/).

8. **W3C WAI, User Notification in Forms.** The tutorial recommends clear success/error feedback and proximity to the relevant controls. REI places tool status by the inputs and announces generated output; paper selection has a count near its export control. This is accessibility implementation guidance, not evidence of learning gains. [Tutorial](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/forms/notifications/).

9. **W3C, Understanding Target Size (Minimum), WCAG 2.2.** The AA criterion discusses minimum target size and spacing exceptions. REI uses comfortably sized section controls, choices and export controls. Checking selected dimensions is not an accessibility audit or a conformance claim. [Criterion explanation](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-minimum).

10. **W3C, Understanding Focus Appearance.** The guidance describes perceivable focus indicators. REI retains visible focus, focuses the chosen section heading after a section jump, and gives generated output a focusable destination. Keyboard behavior needs assistive-technology testing beyond the targeted checks made here. [Criterion explanation](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/focus-appearance).

Sources were reviewed through public publisher/author abstracts, official practice guides, primary-provider guidance and standards pages. Paywalled full chapters were not treated as read. Practitioner heuristics and standards are explicitly distinguished from educational studies. All new scenarios, organizers and feedback text are original REI adaptations; fictional examples do not assert real findings.

## Learning changes inside the same six lessons

- Each lesson has an explicit outcome and one compact decision rule.
- Each worked example keeps its three reasoning moves and adds a four-part organizer: question boundaries, Boolean concepts, a source note, a study comparison, method alignment or claim boundaries.
- Correct-answer positions vary. Learners cannot use “always choose the second option” as a strategy.
- Six additional questions change the context. Their answer explanations distinguish what the available evidence supports and what more would be needed.
- Feedback is local to its own challenge. Trying a second scenario does not overwrite the first response.
- No score, mastery label, countdown, compulsory unlock or streak was introduced. Choices remain session-only.

## What to evaluate in the next pilot

Use participants who have not already learned the previous interface. Keep task wording and facilitator assistance consistent. Observe whether students can identify the lesson’s goal, point to the part of the example that supports a choice, apply the rule in the new scenario, and find the resulting output after using a tool. Ask a content expert to examine the reasoning, not only the chosen option.

Compare the old and refined experience only with a suitable study design if you want causal conclusions. For a usability pilot, record concrete problems and assistance needed. Do not infer learning gains from clicks, time on page or filled fields. A harder second scenario may take longer while producing more useful reasoning.

## Verification scope

Targeted automated and browser results are recorded in HANDOFF.md and the current chat outputs. Real touch hardware, screen readers, other browser engines and educational outcomes are not established by desktop viewport checks. The source API remains external and can fail or be rate limited. Tool drafts and challenge answers still last only for the current session; existing durable work and backups are unchanged.
